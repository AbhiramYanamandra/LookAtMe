## Overview

The brief was a smartwatch. The hardware was UNSW's coaST education board: an **STM32 Nucleo-F303RE** with **four buttons**, a **16×2 character LCD** (thirty-two characters of output, total), a buzzer, a stepper motor, two light-dependent resistors and a row of LEDs. There's no touchscreen, no accelerometer and no heart-rate sensor.

On that we built a home screen, activity tracking (steps, heart rate, floors climbed, blood oxygen), an alarm, a stopwatch, a countdown timer, sound and haptic settings, a solar-charging screen, a flashlight, do-not-disturb and a power-saving mode. We started as a team of three; one member moved groups in week 4, and Samyak Diwan and I finished it as a pair.

## Twenty pages, four buttons

Four buttons can't each have one job, so we weighed two ways to stretch them: short vs long presses, and buttons whose meaning changes with the page. We settled on **context-dependent buttons**, with a long press used only once, on Home, to open the quick-access menu. Navigation is a finite state machine: each page is a state, and each button press is a transition. That keeps every mapping explicit in one place instead of scattered through the input handling.

The cost shows up in the map above. SW1 means *Settings* on Home, *Steps* on Activity, *Alarm* on Clock, and *back* on every settings page. We wrote a user guide built around that navigation map so a new user could find their way, and both markers still flagged navigation as the weakest part: one called it "a steep learning curve" that "requires a map". That's a fair verdict. A map in a manual patches the problem rather than fixing it. Context-dependent buttons need the screen itself to say what each button will do next, which is what the simulator's button list does and the watch didn't.

## Making 32 characters go further

Every page gets two 16-character rows, so we cut words to codes: **HAP**, **SND**, **SLR**, **TIM** on the settings page. Static information like the time stays in fixed positions, and dynamic readings get their own zones. Where a word wouldn't fit at all, we drew it instead: seven custom 5×8 characters stored in the LCD's character RAM, so one cell can say *heart rate* or *battery*.

## Feedback, and sensors we didn't have

Every button press gives feedback twice: a **buzzer** tone, and a short turn of the **stepper motor** as vibration. Each can be switched off in settings. The board had no real health sensors, so the activity readings are simulated. The two **light-dependent resistors** do the rest: the solar-charging screen shows their light readings over the last few minutes, and the blood-oxygen page reuses the same readings. The flashlight lights every LED on the board (D1–D20 and LD2). Do-not-disturb silences alarms and button sounds from the quick-access menu.

## Firmware approach

Our design journal set out a deliberately conservative approach for a small microcontroller:

- **Interrupt-driven buttons** rather than polling.
- **A finite state machine** for navigation.
- **Fixed, statically allocated data**: settings packed as bitfields in a single byte, light readings in an 8-entry rolling buffer indexed with a bit mask, and no dynamic allocation.
- **Integer arithmetic** throughout, avoiding floating point.

<Note>
  These are design choices, described as such. No power or performance measurements were taken, so no low-power claim is made for them.
</Note>

## Assessment

Two tutors marked the viva separately, each out of 100: **74 and 87** (the second an HD). The core features scored full or near-full marks on both sheets: sound and haptics with their settings, the solar-charging screen, the flashlight, the stopwatch and the countdown timer.

The critiques are worth more than the marks:

- **Navigation** was the shared complaint, as above.
- **Editing** could only increase a field, never decrease it.
- **The display** visibly refreshed, which made the timer pages hard to watch. One tutor also found the watch sluggish to respond.
- **The alarm** rang for a full minute and couldn't be stopped, on one marker's run.
- **Advanced features** (do-not-disturb, power saving, the fitness pages) scored 13 and 22 of 30. Power saving only changed what was on screen. It didn't actually save power.

The display and responsiveness problems come down to one fix: redraw only what changed, and handle input before anything else. That's the partial-update plan from our own journal, followed through more strictly.
