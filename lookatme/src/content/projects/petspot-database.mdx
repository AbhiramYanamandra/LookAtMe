## Overview

PETspot.com is an online marketplace where breeders list dogs and dog-related services, and buyers search by breed, coat, size, shedding and location, then enquire and transact. Our team of three designed its database for UTS's Database Fundamentals: case study, business rules, ER model, relations, functional dependencies and normalisation to third normal form, then the seller side built and queried in PostgreSQL.

What makes it a worthwhile modelling exercise rather than a single CRUD table is that it's **multi-sided**. Buyers and sellers are different kinds of account, a seller can list both pets and services, and an enquiry can come from either side and point at either kind of listing.

## From rules to a model

The first draft (Part A) described the product: matching buyers to breeders, application questionnaires and in-site chat. Part B narrowed it to twelve business rules that the database could actually enforce, and Part C revised the model again, adding a pet's parent and litter. ## The decisions in the model

- **Buyers and sellers are separate entities.** Only sellers can post (BR3), and the two are identified differently: a buyer by email address, a seller by a generated identifier (BR2, BR4). Merging them would mean nullable seller-only columns and a role flag doing the work of a schema.
- **Enquiries are entities, not just links.** A buyer can enquire about many pets and a pet can receive many enquiries (BR9), and each enquiry carries its own date-time and status. A many-to-many relationship with attributes of its own needs an associative entity, and there are three: buyer–pet, buyer–service and seller–service.
- **Listings belong to their seller.** Pets and services are keyed by their own ID *plus* the seller's (an identifying relationship), because a pet must be created by exactly one seller (BR6, BR11).
- **A pet can point at a pet.** The optional Parent attribute is a self-referencing foreign key, which is what lets litters be recorded at all (BR5).

## Normalisation

Each business rule became a functional dependency (nine in total), and each relation was checked for atomic attributes (1NF), no partial dependencies on a composite key (2NF), and no dependencies between non-key attributes (3NF). The most important of these was the breed.

## Building it in PostgreSQL

Part D turned the seller side of the model into a working database: **Seller**, **Service** and **Seller_Service_Enquiry**, with primary and foreign keys enforced by PostgreSQL, seeded with 10 sellers, 6 services and 5 enquiries, and queried with a GROUP BY, an INNER JOIN and a subquery. The SQL also settled something the written relations left ambiguous: the enquiry table names its two seller columns by role, with SellerID for the seller asking and ServSellerID for the seller who owns the service.

## What I'd tighten

Re-running the script also surfaced problems that the sample data happened to avoid. Each of these was confirmed against PostgreSQL:

- **One service per seller.** Service.SellerID is declared UNIQUE, so inserting a second service for the same seller fails with a duplicate-key error, which contradicts the model. The composite primary key already guarantees uniqueness, so that UNIQUE should go, and the enquiry's foreign key should then reference (ServiceID, SellerID) together.
- **The script can't be re-run.** It drops Seller first, while Service and the enquiry table still reference it, so the second run stops on a dependency error. Dropping child tables first (or using CASCADE) fixes it.
- **3.a answers a different question.** It asks for the most expensive service by state but averages instead, so WA comes back NULL because its only service has no price. MAX(Cost) answers what was asked.
- **3.c filters on the inside only.** The subquery finds NSW's cheapest price, but the outer query doesn't repeat the state filter, so a service elsewhere at the same price would also be returned.
- **Types worth upgrading.** DateTime is stored as text ('20221015:093015') rather than a TIMESTAMP, and passwords are stored as plain text where a real schema would keep hashes.
- **On the design side**, litters are modelled three ways (a Litter attribute, PET_LITTER and LITTERID) where one LITTER entity would do, and the written PET relation leaves off the breed foreign key that the ERD and FD3 include.
